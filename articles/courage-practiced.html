Courage, Practiced

People talk about courage like weather — something you either have or you don't, that arrives or doesn't. This is wrong in the way that matters most: it makes bravery a lottery instead of a practice. The truth, from every tradition that studied it, is that courage is a skill. Trainable. Progressive. And like every skill in Book Two and Book Four, it responds to deliberate practice.

Aristotle said it first: we become brave by doing brave acts. Not by feeling brave — by acting brave, repeatedly, at increasing difficulty. The feeling follows the act, not the reverse. Waiting to feel courageous before acting is like waiting to feel strong before lifting: the order is backwards, and backwards never works.

The progression

Micro-courage. Start where the stakes are tiny: the disagreement voiced kindly, the question asked in the meeting, the phone call instead of the email. These aren't brave in any heroic sense. They're reps — each one teaching the nervous system that discomfort is survivable and that action is possible inside it.

Named fear. Courage requires knowing what you're afraid of — specifically. Not "I'm anxious" but "I'm afraid they'll think I'm incompetent." The second arrow from Book One lives here: most fear is commentary, not event. Name the actual event. It's almost always smaller than the commentary.

The 10% rule. Don't leap — edge. Each act slightly braver than the last comfortable one. The zone of proximal difficulty from Book Two applies to courage exactly: ~15% beyond comfort is the sweet spot. Too far and you flood; too safe and nothing grows.

The Tuesday-morning version

One brave act today. Small, specific, slightly uncomfortable. The email you've been avoiding. The truth you've been softening. The ask you've been rehearsing instead of making. Do it before noon — courage, like willpower, is fresher early.

Then log it. The notebook from Book Two works here too: what I did, what I feared, what actually happened. Over weeks, the log becomes evidence — your own data proving that the feared outcomes mostly don't occur, and that you survive the ones that do.

Courage isn't the absence of fear. It's the practiced ability to act with the fear present. Every rep makes the next one easier. That's not inspiration. That's training.
