The Spacing Effect

Cramming works. That's the problem — it works just well enough, exactly once, to teach you the wrong lesson. You stay up, you pour it in, you pass the test, and by next month it's gone like it was never there. Because it wasn't, really. It was in short-term storage, and short-term storage gets emptied.

The alternative was discovered over a century ago and has been confirmed so many times it's embarrassing we still teach any other way: the spacing effect. Spread the same study time across days, and you remember dramatically more than if you mass it into one session. Same hours, multiplied results.

The forgetting curve

Hermann Ebbinghaus, in the 1880s, memorized thousands of nonsense syllables and measured how fast they decayed. The curve he found is brutal: without review, you lose most of it within a day. But — and this is the whole game — each review flattens the curve. Review at the right moment, just as you're about to forget, and the memory consolidates deeper each time. After enough spaced reviews, it's yours for years.

This is why the ancient memory masters could do what they did. The "memory palace" — the method of loci, used by Roman orators to deliver hours-long speeches without notes — works by giving abstract material vivid spatial hooks: walk through a familiar house, place each item in a room, walk the route to retrieve. It feels like a trick. It's actually spaced, elaborative encoding — the brain's preferred format.

The Tuesday-morning version

One: never study the same thing twice in one day. Split it. An hour today and an hour Thursday beats two hours today, every time, for anything you want to keep.

Two: review at the edge of forgetting. The best moment to review is when it's slightly hard to recall — not fresh, not gone. That effortful retrieval is what builds the trace. Flashcards (physical or digital) with expanding intervals do this automatically: get it right, see it later; get it wrong, see it sooner.

Three: sleep on it. Memory consolidates during sleep — the brain replays the day's learning and files it. The all-nighter doesn't just tire you; it skips the filing. Study, sleep, review: that's the loop.

Cramming is borrowing against tomorrow's memory to pay for today's test. Spacing is investing. Same principal, compound interest.
