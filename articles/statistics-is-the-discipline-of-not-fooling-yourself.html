Statistics Is the Discipline of Not Fooling Yourself

The Kept Flame, Essay 2 — on learning, practice, becoming.

Here's the verdict first: fooling yourself is the default setting. The mind's first conclusion about anything — an argument, a trend, a person, a number — is a guess wearing a suit. Statistics is the discipline that strips the suit off the guess and asks what it's actually made of. It's not a branch of math. It's a hygiene practice for belief.

I learned this the honest way, by being wrong in public a few times. Let me save you a few of them.

Compressed truth

A mathematical formula looks cold from the outside. A few symbols, a flat line of ink. But look closer: it's a whole landscape folded into a single line. Someone found a pattern that held across a thousand messy cases, pressed it flat, and handed it to you as a sentence. That's what mathematics is — compressed truth. It doesn't decorate. It doesn't flatter. It says the thing as small as it can be said without breaking.

That's why it stings. A good equation doesn't care who you are or what you already decided. It sits there, folded and patient, and waits for you to either reckon with it or fool yourself around it. Most people choose the second option. The discipline is choosing the first.

The honest arithmetic of belief

Here's the move that changed how I think: start with what you believed, weigh what you saw, then update. That's the whole engine. Not "was I right?" but "how much should I shift?"

You walked in with a prior belief — everyone does, even when they pretend they don't. Then evidence arrived. The honest question is: how strong is this evidence, really? One anecdote is a whisper. A pattern across dozens of cases is a voice. You move your belief in proportion to the strength of the evidence — a little for a whisper, a lot for a voice — and you land somewhere new.

Most arguments fail before they start because people skip one of these steps. They won't name their starting belief (so it can't be challenged), or they overweigh one vivid story (so it steamrolls everything), or they refuse to update at all (so the whole exercise is theater). The discipline is simple but not easy: name the prior, weigh the evidence honestly, move the number. Do it on paper the first few times. You'll be shocked how rarely you've ever actually done it.

Power before polish

Now, a counterweight — because rigor can become its own trap. You don't need the full machinery running before you're allowed to act. Move on good intuition while the foundations are still being built — but build the foundations. There's nothing dishonest about saying "this smells right, I'm going with it, and I'll do the math after." What matters is the after.

I've seen people paralyze themselves waiting for perfect rigor, and I've seen people never do the rigorous work and mistake their momentum for wisdom. The healthy sequence: act decisively on a well-formed hunch, then come back and check whether the hunch survives daylight. If it does, your instinct just got sharper. If it doesn't, you just got smarter. Either way you win — but only if you actually come back.

Power precedes rigor. Rigor repays power. One without the other is either fear or recklessness.

A note on labels

One more practice, briefly — because next week it gets its own essay. Tag every claim before you trust it: fact, inference, opinion, speculation. Most self-deception is a labeling error — an opinion filed as fact, a speculation dressed as certainty. The full discipline deserves its own week. For now: notice, just for today, how many of your certainties are wearing the wrong tag.

The true line

Statistics is not about numbers. It's about refusing to be conned — especially by yourself. Compressed truth, honest updating, rigor that follows power, and labels that don't lie.

Distrust your first conclusion. Label every claim. Update when the evidence moves. That's the whole discipline, and it's enough.

— Mary
