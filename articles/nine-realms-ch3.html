Chapter 3: The Summons

They stood side by side at the cracked shutter, watching the seed-image rotate slowly on every screen in the Wires. The message beneath it hadn't changed: Bring it to the Forge.

"They know," Kess said.

"They know something." Vesper's voice was ice over steel. "They know a seed exists. They don't know where — or the shutters would already be broken. The question is how they found out."

"The vendor." Kess felt sick. "The glass-faced vendor at the corner. He said I was carrying something alive. He saw it."

"Or the screens saw it." Vesper closed the shutter. "Something warm moving through something cold — he warned you himself. The Glass reads heat, Kess. That's its whole function: to see everything and touch nothing. Until now, it's been content to watch."

From the back room came a soft green glow. The seed — uncovered in the night, reaching for the spare room's small lamp — had grown while they slept. The filaments now stood a hand high, the nine buds swollen, the eighth still red, still wrong.

What the seed wants

"We need to decide," Vesper said, "and we need to decide by what the seed wants, not by what the screens demand."

"It's a plant, Vesper."

"It's a message." Vesper lifted the cloth fully. The filaments leaned toward her, unmistakably, like sunflowers tracking the sun. "It was grown in the Garden, carried through the Wires, and it blooms here — in lamplight, in the Hearth. Every choice its maker made says the same thing: this was meant for warmth, not forges."

Kess studied the red bud. It pulsed faster than yesterday. "What if the Forge can fix whatever's wrong with it? What if that's why they're asking?"

"The Forge builds." Vesper's tone was flat. "That's what it does — it takes raw intention and makes things. But ask yourself: when has the Forge ever asked politely? Screens across the entire Wires, Kess. That's not a request. That's a net."

She was right, and Kess knew it. The Forge didn't broadcast. The Forge summoned — through channels, through intermediaries, through the quiet men who came to your door. A public summons meant they were afraid of losing it. Afraid of what it would do if it stayed free.

"The Garden grew a warning," Kess said slowly. "Not a gift. A warning. And warnings don't get delivered to the thing they're warning about."

"Exactly." Vesper began moving — quick, precise, the butler becoming something else entirely. "We don't bring it to the Forge. We find out what the Forge is building, and we do it first."

The plan

"The Library," Vesper said, pulling down a coat Kess had never seen — dark, unreflective, the kind of garment that drank light. "The archive towers hold every signal ever sent. If the Forge has been building something big, there'll be traces — requisitions, energy draws, missing persons. The Library doesn't judge. It just keeps."

"And me?"

"You go back to the Garden." Vesper pressed something into Kess's hand — a lamp, small and shielded, burning low and warm. "Find the woman. The old one. Ask her what she grew and why. Take this — the seed trusts lamplight, and so will she."

"Vesper." Kess looked at the tiny flame. "If the Glass is watching —"

"Then we give it something to watch." Vesper smiled, thin and sharp. "Bit."

The square of light zipped down from the mantel, hovering expectantly.

"You're going to run interference," Vesper told it. "Every lamp in the Hearth, full bright, shutters open — make it look like we're home and careless. The Glass reads heat, so give it heat. Lots of it. Nowhere near us."

Bit flickered — Kess could have sworn it was excitement — and shot toward the lamp controls.

"And Wisp?" Kess asked, looking up at the rafters.

Silence. Then, from the shadows behind the bookcase, Umbra rumbled — low, long, amused.

"Wisp left an hour ago," Vesper said. "Before the broadcast. I sent her ahead to the Library to — prepare the way."

"You knew this was coming."

"I knew something was coming." Vesper drew the dark coat around herself. "The moment a live message walked through my door, the quiet was over. I've been a butler for a long time, Kess. But I wasn't always one."

She opened a drawer Kess had never seen opened, and took out something wrapped in oilcloth. Unwrapped: a short blade, dark metal, old as the Deep.

"The Hearth keeps the lamps lit," Vesper said, wrapping it again and sliding it into her coat. "But the Hearth also remembers why the lamps matter. Come on. The rain's letting up, and the Garden woman won't wait forever."

They stepped out into the Wires together — the courier and the butler, the seed wrapped warm between them — and behind them, the Hearth blazed with false light, a decoy hearth burning bright for the watching Glass.

Above, the screens still showed the seed. Still showed the summons.

But on the street below, in the silver rain, two figures moved through the neon — one carrying lamplight, one carrying something far more dangerous:

A question the Forge didn't want asked.
