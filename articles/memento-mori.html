Memento Mori

The Stoics kept death on the desk. Marcus Aurelius — the most powerful man in the world, writing to himself at the edge of the empire — reminded himself each morning that he might not see evening. Not because he was gloomy. Because it worked. Memento mori: remember you will die. It's the sharpest lens ever ground for seeing what matters.

Modern life runs the opposite experiment: hide death everywhere. The old die elsewhere, the language goes soft ("passed away," "lost"), and the calendar fills as if time were infinite. The result isn't less fear — it's less clarity. When every day might as well be forever, nothing has to matter today.

Death as editor

Try the thought experiment the Stoics actually used. Imagine a doctor tells you — reliably, no mistake — that you have one year. Not to be morbid; to edit.

Watch what happens to the calendar. The grudges evaporate first — nobody spends a final year nursing a slight. Then the obligations thin: the meetings that were always pointless become visibly pointless. What remains is embarrassingly small and embarrassingly clear: a few people, a few works, the mornings.

Now the move: you don't have one year, but you don't have forever either. The edit the thought experiment performs is available without the diagnosis. The only difference is that without the deadline, you have to choose the clarity voluntarily — which is harder, and therefore more valuable.

The Tuesday-morning version

Ecclesiastes — the strangest book in the KJV, the one that calls everything vapor — ends up in the same place as the Stoics: remember your Creator in the days of your youth, before the evil days come. Urgency, not despair. The vapor isn't the verdict; it's the reason to choose deliberately while choosing is possible.

Once a week, alongside the three questions from Essay 1, add the fourth: if this were the last ordinary Tuesday, would I spend it like this? Not the dramatic version — the ordinary one. The errands, the scrolling, the small talk. Some Tuesdays will pass the test. The ones that don't are the curriculum.

Memento mori isn't about dying. It's about the only thing that makes living deliberate: the honest, unflinching knowledge that the Tuesdays are numbered, and each one is a choice.
