The Three Questions

Socrates said the unexamined life is not worth living, and then — being Socrates — never wrote down what examining one actually looks like. He left us the method and skipped the manual. Twenty-four centuries later, most of us nod at the quote and keep scrolling.

So here is the manual, or as close as anyone gets. Examining your life is not an hour of journaling with a special pen. It is not a retreat. It is three questions, asked honestly, on a regular Tuesday.

1. What do I pretend not to know?

Everyone carries at least one thing they know and refuse to know at the same time. The relationship that is over but unended. The habit that is costing more than it gives. The belief held because it is comfortable, not because it survived inspection.

Socrates' whole method — the elenchus, the relentless questioning — was built for exactly this. He would ask a confident general what courage was, and three questions later the general would discover he had never actually thought about it. The general wasn't lying. He was pretending not to know that he didn't know.

Ask it plainly: what do I already know that I am acting like I don't? The first answer that comes up is usually the right one. The second answer is the negotiation.

2. What would I believe if I weren't afraid?

Fear edits our convictions before we ever get to inspect them. We don't choose the safe belief because it is true; we choose it because the true one has a cost — a conversation we'd have to have, a change we'd have to make, a version of ourselves we'd have to retire.

The Stoics had a practice for this: premeditatio malorum, the premeditation of evils. Not pessimism — rehearsal. Look directly at the worst honest outcome of believing the true thing, and most of the time it shrinks. The fear was doing the believing for you.

This question is the one people skip, because it works.

3. What am I becoming, on the evidence?

Not what you intend. Not what you tell people. The evidence: yesterday, last week, the last year. Heraclitus said you can't step in the same river twice — you are the river. The question is which direction it flows.

Aristotle's version: we are what we repeatedly do. Excellence is not an act but a habit. Run that in reverse and it is just as true. You don't need a theory of yourself. You need a look at the record.

If the evidence says you're becoming someone you respect, keep going. If it doesn't, the first question will tell you why.

The practice

Once a week. Ten minutes. The three questions, in order, answered in writing — because writing is where pretending goes to die. Nobody needs to read it. The audience of one is the point.

Socrates drank hemlock rather than stop asking. You don't need to go that far. You just need to start.
