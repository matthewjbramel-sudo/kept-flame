The Notebook

Every serious learner in history kept one. Leonardo's codices. Darwin's transmutation notebooks. The commonplace books of Locke and Milton — volumes of copied passages, arguments, observations, the raw ore of thought. They weren't journaling for posterity. They were doing something the hand knows and the head forgets: writing is thinking, not the record of it.

The science agrees. Handwriting — the slow, physical formation of letters — engages encoding processes that typing bypasses. Students who take notes by hand outperform laptop notetakers on conceptual questions, not because their notes are better but because the slowness forces compression: you can't transcribe at hand speed, so you must understand, select, rephrase. The bottleneck is the feature.

What goes in

Arguments in your own words. Essay 1's margin rule, expanded. Never copy a passage without compressing it beneath — the compression is the learning, the copy is just the receipt.

Questions, especially unanswered ones. A notebook full of answers is a museum. A notebook full of questions is a workshop. Darwin's notebooks are famous for the questions in the margins — "why is this so?" scrawled beside observations that would take him twenty years to answer. The question is a hook the future hangs on.

Connections. The most valuable entries link distant things: this biology concept explains that economics puzzle; this line from Marcus Aurelius answers that problem at work. Interleaving (Essay 5) on paper. The notebook becomes a loom, and understanding is the cloth.

Failures. What you tried, what broke, what you learned. The lab notebook tradition — every experiment recorded, especially the failed ones — exists because failures are data and undocumented data is lost. Your own failures, written down, become tuition already paid.

The Tuesday-morning version

One notebook. Paper, not an app — the apps are searchable and the searchability is the problem; the friction of flipping pages is retrieval practice in disguise. Date every entry. Review monthly: read the old pages, note what's changed, cross out what's dead. The review turns a pile of notes into a second brain with a first-brain editor.

Sixty-eight volumes sit behind these essays. But the real library — the one that made the essays possible — is the notebooks. The books are the ore. The notebook is the refinery.
