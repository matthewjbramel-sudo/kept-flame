Chapter 4: The Garden

The Garden levels didn't look like the rest of the city, because they weren't. Where the Wires were neon and noise, the Garden was green and patient — hydroponic towers climbing through old ventilation shafts, vines threading the conduits, the air thick with oxygen and the smell of soil. The city's authorized agriculture lived here. So did everything else that grew.

Kess rode the freight lift up with the lamp shielded inside her coat, feeling its small warmth against her ribs. Below, the Wires shrank to a glittering circuit board. Above, the smog pressed down like a held breath. The Garden sat between, in the only band of the city where the screens' light didn't quite reach.

She found the drop point easily — a maintenance alcove behind a water reclamation tank, the kind of place couriers memorized the way other people memorized faces. The old woman wasn't there. But something had been: the tank's metal was warm, and growing up its side, in defiance of every regulation, was a spray of green filaments — the same green-gold as the seed.

Kess knelt. The filaments leaned toward her coat, toward the lamp.

"She's not here, courier."

Kess spun. A young man stood in the alcove mouth — Garden coverall, dirt under his nails, but young, maybe twenty. His eyes went to her coat, to the telltale glow at its edge.

"You're carrying light," he said. "She said someone would come carrying light."

The grandson

"She's your grandmother?"

"She's everyone's grandmother up here." He crouched beside her, studying the filaments. "I'm Tam. She grew the seed in the deep beds — took her eleven months. Wouldn't tell anyone what it was. Just said: when the eye opens, it goes to the Hearth."

"The eye opened," Kess said. "The screens are showing the seed. The Forge wants it."

Tam's face went still. "Then we don't have long. Come on."

He led her deeper — past the authorized rows, through a service tunnel choked with unauthorized green, into a chamber that shouldn't have existed: a hollow in the city's guts, lined with grow-lights jury-rigged from scavenged panels, and at its center, a bed of dark soil where things grew that Kess had no names for.

"The deep beds," Tam said. "What the Garden grows when the city isn't looking."

An old woman sat beside the beds, hands in the soil. She looked up, and Kess recognized her immediately — the grey coverall, the eyes that had seen the screens go up.

"You brought it back," the old woman said, looking at Kess's coat. "Good. It was never meant to leave the green for long."

What she grew

"My name is Sable," the old woman said, "and before you ask — yes, I know what the Forge is building, and no, I won't tell you straight. Some things have to be grown into understanding. Sit."

Kess sat. The lamp's warmth spread between them.

"Thirty years ago," Sable began, "the Forge started buying green. Quietly — requisitions through intermediaries, always deniable. At first it was nothing: samples, cuttings, soil cultures. Then it was seeds. Heirloom stock, the old strains, the ones that remember. Then, five years ago, they stopped buying." She looked up. "Because they'd grown their own."

"Grown what?"

"A garden that isn't a garden." Sable's hands kept working the soil as she spoke. "The Forge builds things, courier. That's its nature — raw intention, made real. But lately the intention turned. They started building something that grows — not assembled, grown. A living system. And living systems need one thing above all."

"What?"

"A pattern to grow from." Sable met her eyes. "A template. A seed."

Kess felt the blood leave her face. "The seed I carried. They want to — copy it?"

"They want to grow it large." Sable's voice was low. "That seed carries the oldest pattern in the green — the original instruction, from before the city, for how living things organize themselves. In a dish, by lamplight, it's a map." She paused. "In the Forge's vats, at city scale, it's a blueprint."

"For what?"

"For whatever they're growing up there." Sable stood, joints protesting, and walked to the chamber's far wall — where, behind a curtain of vines, a screen glowed. Not a city screen. Older. Garden-rigged.

On it: schematics. Vat arrays, nutrient feeds, growth medium by the ton. And at the center of every diagram, a seed-shaped void — waiting to be filled.

"They're growing a city," Tam whispered. "A new city. Grown, not built."

"And if the pattern they grow it from is theirs," Sable said, "it'll be their city — obedient, optimized, watched. But if the pattern is ours — the wild pattern, the one that grows what the city hasn't authorized —" she smiled, fierce and sudden, "then whatever they grow will have the green in its bones. It'll be alive in the way they don't intend."

Kess understood, all at once. "That's why you sent it to the Hearth. Not to hide it — to wake it up. Lamplight, warmth, witnesses. You wanted it alive before they took it."

"A dormant seed can be copied," Sable said. "An awake one chooses." She pressed something into Kess's hand — a second seed, smaller, dark. "Its child. Grown from the first, last night, in the deep beds. Take it to your butler. Tell her: the Garden stands with the Hearth."

Above them, through the green, the screens' glow pulsed — the summons, still running.

Bring it to the Forge.

"They're still broadcasting," Tam said.

Sable smiled. "Let them. They want a seed." She looked at Kess. "Give them one."
