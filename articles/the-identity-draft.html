The Identity Draft

Most people treat identity like a verdict — this is who I am, handed down once, appeal denied. The shy one stays shy. The bad-at-math one stays bad. The person who "isn't a morning person" schedules their life around a sentence someone said about them at nineteen. But identity isn't a verdict. It's a draft. And drafts get revised.

Psychology backs this: the "end of history illusion" — we recognize how much we've changed in the last ten years while insisting we won't change much in the next ten. We're always wrong. The person you are now is a rough draft of someone you haven't met, and the revision never stops unless you stop it.

What keeps the draft frozen

The story. "I'm just not a ___ person." Every blank filled becomes a cage. The sentence feels like self-knowledge; it's usually just repetition — something said so often it hardened. Ask where the sentence came from. If the answer is older than five years, it's overdue for review.

The audience. People around you have a version of you filed, and they enforce it — subtly, constantly. Change too fast and they get uncomfortable, because your revision implies their draft might need one too. This is why becoming often requires new rooms, or at least new conversations in old rooms.

The sunk cost. "I've always been this way" is the identity version of throwing good money after bad. Years invested in a self don't make it true. They just make it expensive to question — which is exactly why questioning it matters.

The Tuesday-morning version

Pick one identity sentence you carry — I'm not creative, I'm bad with people, I can't stick with things. Then run the draft test: Is it data or story? Data has recent evidence; story has old repetition. What would the next draft look like? Not the final version — just the next revision, one degree truer. What's one action the next draft would take this week? Identity follows behavior more reliably than behavior follows identity. Act like the draft, and the draft becomes you.

Nietzsche's command from Book One — become who you are — was never about discovering a hidden true self. It was about authoring one. You are not a finished thing. You are a draft. Pick up the pen.
