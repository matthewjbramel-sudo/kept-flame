Mix It Up

The standard way to practice is blocking: drill skill A until it's smooth, then skill B, then skill C. A-A-A, B-B-B, C-C-C. It feels like progress — each block gets easier as you groove the pattern. Then the test mixes everything, and the groove turns out to have been a rut.

The better way is interleaving: A, B, C, A, C, B. Mix the skills within each session. Practice feels worse — you're constantly switching, never settling into the groove. Test performance is better, often dramatically. In one classic study, interleaved math students scored nearly twice as high on delayed tests as blocked students, despite feeling less confident during practice.

Why mixing wins

Blocking teaches execution. Interleaving teaches discrimination — which tool for which problem. Real life never announces "this is a type-B situation." The doctor sees symptoms, not labels. The negotiator reads rooms, not scripts. The test of skill was never "can you do it when cued" — it's "can you recognize what the moment needs."

Every switch also forces a fresh retrieval — Essay 1's mechanism again. Blocked practice retrieves once and coasts on working memory. Interleaved practice retrieves every time, because the previous problem was something else entirely. The friction is the learning, as Essay 4 established.

The Tuesday-morning version

Study: don't finish one chapter before starting the next. Rotate subjects within a session — twenty minutes each, shuffled. Your notes will look messier. Your memory won't.

Skills: musicians know this as varied practice; athletes as random practice. The tennis player who drills forehands for an hour grooves a forehand. The one who mixes forehands, backhands, and volleys learns tennis.

Work: batching similar tasks feels efficient, and for pure throughput it is. But for learning a craft — writing, coding, designing — interleaved projects build the judgment that blocked repetition never touches: not just how, but when and which.

The groove feels like mastery. It's actually the absence of decisions. Mix it up, and every rep becomes a decision — which is what the real performance was always going to demand.
