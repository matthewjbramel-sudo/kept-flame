The Art of Quitting

We're taught that quitting is failure — winners never quit. This is one of the most expensive lies in circulation. The truth: quitting is a skill, and the people who never learn it spend years funding dead projects, dead relationships, and dead identities with fresh effort. The sunk cost fallacy isn't a cognitive bias you read about. It's a tax you pay daily.

The fallacy is simple: I've invested so much, I can't stop now. But invested costs are gone — that's what sunk means. The only question that matters is forward-looking: given where I am now, is the next unit of effort worth it? The past is data, not a down payment on the future.

When to quit

The plateau test. From Book Two: plateaus are normal, and most break with deliberate practice. But some plateaus are information — the market telling you, the body telling you, the results telling you. The difference: a practice plateau responds to changed method; a dead-end plateau doesn't respond to anything. If you've varied the approach three times and nothing moves, it's not a plateau. It's a wall.

The identity test. From Essay 1: are you continuing because the next draft of you does this, or because the old draft can't admit it was wrong? Sunk identity is the hardest sunk cost — quitting feels like confessing the years were wasted. They weren't. They were tuition. But tuition already paid doesn't obligate you to keep attending.

The opportunity test. Every yes is a thousand nos. The project you're grinding isn't just costing effort — it's costing everything else you could be doing with that effort. Ask: if I weren't already doing this, would I start it today? If the answer is no, you're funding the past with the future.

How to quit well

Quitting isn't flouncing. Decide by criteria, not mood. Set the kill conditions in advance: if X hasn't happened by Y, I stop. Deciding mid-frustration produces both premature quits and zombie projects. Quit clean. Close it properly — the conversation, the handoff, the archive. Messy exits tax the next thing. Extract the tuition. Write down what the years taught before you walk away. The notebook rule: undocumented failure is just failure; documented failure is education.

Strategic quitting isn't the opposite of perseverance. It's what makes perseverance affordable — because every dead thing you stop funding is fuel for the live ones.
