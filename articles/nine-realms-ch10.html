Chapter 10: The Warden Remembers

It started with a petal.

Kess found it on her morning run — a white petal from one of the screen-flowers, lying on the wet pavement of the Wires, edges brown. She picked it up. It crumbled. Behind her, the screen it had fallen from flickered — and for half a second, beneath the flowers, she saw the old glass gleam through. Cold. Watching.

By week's end, half the screen-flowers were browning. The green-gold light in the towers dimmed in patches, like a lamp running low. And the Glass — the Glass was learning. The summons didn't return. Something quieter took its place: a watching with intent behind it, the way a predator watches before the lunge.

"It's remembering," Vesper said. They stood on the Hearth's roof at dusk, watching the patches of dim spread. "The bloom changed its nature. But nature — even changed nature — has habits. The Glass was a warden for a hundred years before it was a gardener for a month."

"Can the bloom hold?" Kess asked.

Vesper didn't answer for a long time. Then: "The bloom is a guest in the Glass's house. Guests can be asked to leave."

The Forge's old guard

Rowan brought the worse news, arriving at the Hearth at midnight with soil still under their nails and fear in their clear eyes.

"The Cradle's council met," Rowan said. "The old engineers — the ones who built the copy, the ones who lost when it bloomed wild. They've been quiet for weeks. They're done being quiet."

"What do they want?" Vesper asked.

"To cut it out." Rowan's hands shook. "The bloom threaded through the whole sector — the towers, the screens, the Cradle itself. They can't remove it without —" They stopped.

"Without what?"

"Without killing the sector's power grid. The bloom is the grid now, in places. It grew into the conduits. Cutting it out means dark towers, dead screens, cold vats. They're willing." Rowan looked up. "They're calling it pruning. Like the city is a garden and the wild part is weeds."

Bit flickered — a sharp, angry pulse. Wisp translated, very quietly: "Bit says pruning is what you do to things you own."

"When?" Vesper asked.

"Three days. The equinox — the grid's lowest load. They'll cut the sector, burn out the bloom's roots with a power surge, and regrow from the stored pattern." Rowan's voice dropped. "The tame one. They kept a copy of the copy."

What Vesper carried

After Rowan left, Kess found Vesper on the roof, unwrapping the blade for the first time since Kess had known her.

The blade was Forge-made. The maker's mark was unmistakable — the same mark stamped on the Cradle's gantry steel.

"You were Forge," Kess said.

"I was their blade." Vesper didn't look up. "Before the Hearth. Before the coat got its current color. I cut things for them — problems, people, patterns they didn't like. I was very good at it." She rewrapped the blade with practiced hands. "I left when they asked me to cut something I wouldn't. A gardener. From the deep beds."

"Sable?"

"Sable's daughter." Vesper finally looked up, and her eyes were old. "She's buried in the Quiet Country, where the signal doesn't reach. Where they couldn't —" She stopped. "That's why the Hearth exists, Kess. That's why the lamps burn. Someone has to keep a light on for the things the Forge wants to cut."

Kess sat beside her. Below, the Hearth glowed — the gathering place, the open door, Bit's lamps warm against the dimming green.

"Three days," Kess said.

"Three days." Vesper's hand found the wrapped blade. "And this time, I'm not running."
