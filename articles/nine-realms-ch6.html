Chapter 6: The Decoy

Kess rode the freight lift down with two seeds and no good options.

In her left pocket: the original — awake, blooming, nine buds, one red, the map that had chosen lamplight and the Hearth. In her right pocket: its child — dormant, dark, ungrown, given by Sable with the words give them one.

The plan had formed on the way down, the way plans did for couriers — not as strategy but as route. The Forge wanted a seed. The screens demanded a seed. So: give them a seed. The dormant one — the child, ungrown, unawakened. A seed that had never seen lamplight, never bloomed, never chosen. Let the Forge grow that in their Cradle and see what comes up.

It was a good plan. It was also, Kess knew, the kind of plan that got couriers read.

The Hearth, burning

She came down into the Wires at dusk — the hour when the neon took over from the day and the screens burned brightest. The Hearth blazed ahead of her, every lamp at full, shutters open, exactly as Vesper had ordered: a decoy hearth, burning bright for the watching Glass.

Too bright, she realized, as she got closer. The light was wrong — not lamplight-warm but white-hot, the full blaze of every lamp Bit could drive. And the door stood open.

Kess broke into a run.

Inside: chaos, but the organized kind. Bit zipped between lamps like a panicked star. The furniture was shoved aside. And in the center of the room, where the dish had been — empty. The seed, the filaments, the nine buds: gone.

"Bit!" Kess shouted. "What happened?"

Bit flickered — no words, never words — but its light pulsed in a pattern Kess had learned to read over three years of runs: taken. Not by force. By — and then a flicker she didn't know.

"Slowly," she said. "Who took it?"

Bit dimmed, brightened, dimmed — and then projected, faintly, onto the far wall: a shape. A figure in a Garden coverall. Dirt under the nails. But wrong — the movements too smooth, the eyes —

"The Chief Cultivation Officer," Kess breathed. "The one the Forge took from the Garden. Five years ago."

Bit pulsed confirmation.

The choice

Kess's hand went to her pockets. Left: the original — no. Gone. Taken. Her fingers found only lint where the awake seed had been.

Wait. She checked again, carefully. Left pocket: empty. Right pocket —

The child seed. Still there. Dark, dormant, ungrown.

And then she understood, all at once, with the cold clarity that came to couriers in bad moments: the thief had taken the wrong one. Or the right one, depending on whose side you were on. They'd taken the awake seed — the bloomed map, the one that had chosen the Hearth.

Unless —

Kess pulled out the child seed and looked at it. Dark. Still. Dormant.

Give them one, Sable had said.

Had Sable known? Had the old woman planned this — sent Kess down with two seeds, knowing the Forge would come for the Hearth, knowing they'd take the obvious one? The awake seed was the map. But the child — grown from the first, in the deep beds, last night — what was it?

Bit was flickering urgently now, pointing at the door — no, through the door, at the street. Kess looked.

Vesper was coming up the street at a run — coat flying, Wisp a bright streak above her — and behind her, in the rain-silvered dark, shapes moved. Not police. Not Forge security. Something else: figures in unmarked coveralls, moving with the too-smooth gait of the cultivated.

Kess made the decision the way couriers did — not by thinking but by moving. She crossed to the brightest lamp, the one Bit had driven to white heat, and held the child seed up to it.

"Wake up," she whispered. "Please. Whatever you are — wake up."

For a heartbeat, nothing. Then — a pulse. Faint, warm, green.

The child seed cracked — and what emerged wasn't filaments. It was a single tendril, bright as new leaves, that wrapped around Kess's finger like a handshake.

The door burst open. Vesper stood there, breathing hard, blade in her hand — and stopped dead at the sight: Kess, the lamp, the green tendril curled around her finger like it had been waiting for her all along.

"The Forge has the map," Kess said quickly. "They took the awake one. But Sable gave me this — its child. And I think —" the tendril tightened gently, "I think it chose."

Vesper looked from the seed to the street, where the shapes were closing in, to Bit — who had gone very still, very bright.

"Then we run," Vesper said, "and we run now, and we run to the one place the Glass can't follow."

"Where?"

Vesper smiled — fierce, sudden, alive.

"The Quiet Country. Where the signal doesn't reach."

Behind them, the Hearth's lamps flared one last time — Bit's farewell, bright as a small sun — and then went dark, all at once, leaving the watchers staring at an empty room, an empty dish, and the fading smell of lamplight.
