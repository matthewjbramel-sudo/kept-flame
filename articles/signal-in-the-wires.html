Prologue: Signal in the Wires

The rain in the Wires never stopped. It wasn't water — nothing in the Wires was anything so honest — it was static bleed, runoff from the million signals overhead, and it came down in silver sheets that hissed where they hit the hot neon. Kess pulled her coat tighter and kept moving. Couriers who stopped in the Wires got read, and getting read got you rewritten.

The message in her pocket was warm. That was the problem with this one. Messages were supposed to be cold — dead weight, sealed, none of her business. This one pulsed against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and every few blocks it skipped.

"You're carrying something alive," said the vendor at the corner stall, without looking up from his grill. His face was half glass — not a mask, a window. Behind it, circuitry breathed. "That's a mistake, courier."

"Everything's a mistake down here," Kess said. "What's your point?"

"My point is the Glass is watching." He nodded upward, where the great screens hung between the towers like patient moons. "Something warm moving through something cold. They'll notice."

She noticed the screens too. Everyone did. That was their function.

The nine

The old-timers said the city had nine layers, nine realms stacked like sediment, and Kess had walked enough of them to believe it.

Below: the Deep, where the first cables were laid and something down there still dreamed in voltage. The Ash, the burnt-out sectors, quiet as a held breath. The Quiet Country, where the signal didn't reach and people went when they wanted to be unfindable.

At the middle: the Wires themselves — the neon sprawl, the market, the noise — and the Glass, the world behind the screens, where you could see everything and touch nothing. And between them, the Hearth: the one warm room in the cold city, where Vesper kept the lamps lit and the tea hot and asked no questions that mattered.

Above: the Library, the archive towers where every signal ever sent was stored and indexed. The Garden, the green levels, where things grew that the city hadn't authorized. And the Forge, high as the smog allowed, where new things were built out of raw intention and old parts.

Nine realms. One city. Kess ran messages between all of them, and asked nothing, and that had worked for three years.

Until the warm message.

The Hearth

Vesper's door was unmarked, the way all important doors were. Kess knocked twice — the courier's knock — and the door opened before her knuckles left the wood.

"You're early," Vesper said. She was precise the way a blade is precise: still, bright, and not to be handled carelessly. Behind her, the Hearth glowed — real lamplight, real wood, the only room in the Wires that didn't hum. A small square of light zipped along the mantel, adjusting the lamps. In the corner, something large shifted in the shadows and rumbled, low.

"I'm not early," Kess said. "I'm compromised." She held out the message. It pulsed in her palm, warm as a coal. "It's alive, Vesper. What do I do with a live one?"

Vesper looked at the message for a long moment. Then she did something Kess had never seen her do: she closed the door, turned the lock, and drew every blind in the Hearth.

"You bring it inside," Vesper said quietly, "and you tell me who gave it to you. Every detail. Because a live message in the Wires means someone upstairs in the Forge is building something — and whatever they're building, the Glass already knows."

Outside, the rain kept falling. On the great screens between the towers, something flickered — just for a second — like an eye opening.

Kess stepped inside, and Vesper shut out the neon.
