Steelmen, Not Strawmen

There is a move in argument that feels like winning and teaches you nothing: take the other side's weakest version, knock it down, and walk away confirmed. Everyone does it. It has a name — the strawman — and it is the most common intellectual fraud in circulation, committed daily by people who would never steal a dollar.

The alternative has a name too: the principle of charity. Before you reject an argument you dislike, reconstruct it in its strongest form. Steelman it. Then — and only then — decide what you think.

Why the weak version is tempting

Strawmanning works because it is efficient. The flattened version of your opponent fits in a sentence, dies in two, and leaves you feeling sharp. But notice what happened: you never actually encountered the other mind. You fought a scarecrow wearing its clothes.

Socrates understood this twenty-four centuries ago. His method — the elenchus — only works on real positions. Ask a man what courage is, and you have to let him give his actual answer, not the version convenient to refute. The whole point is the collision with something you didn't expect.

What steelmanning actually requires

Three steps, none of them comfortable:

First, state their case so well they would sign it. Not your summary — theirs. If they read your version and said "that's not what I meant," you haven't started. This alone eliminates most disagreements, because most disagreements are about different sentences, not different thoughts.

Second, find the strongest evidence for it. Not the evidence you can dismiss — the evidence that genuinely troubles your position. If nothing troubles you, you haven't looked. The philosophy volume on my shelf puts it bluntly: whenever a thinker presents an argument you dislike, reconstruct it at full strength before rejecting it. Straw men teach nothing.

Third, then decide. And notice: sometimes, after the steelman, you change your mind. That is not losing. That is the entire point of having a mind — to be the kind of thing that can be improved by contact with other minds.

The Tuesday-morning version

You don't need a debate stage. The next time someone says something that makes you flinch — a coworker, a headline, a relative at dinner — pause before the rebuttal forms. Ask: what would this person have to believe for this to be reasonable? Then argue with that.

Most of the time, you'll find the reasonable version is closer to your own view than the flinch suggested. The remaining distance is the real disagreement, and it is almost always smaller — and more interesting — than the strawman war.

Your opponents are smarter than you think. Not because they are, necessarily. Because you haven't met them yet.
