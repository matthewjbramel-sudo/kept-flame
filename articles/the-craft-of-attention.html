The Craft of Attention

There's a paradox at the center of modern work: the most valuable skill is the ability to focus deeply, and the environment is engineered to prevent exactly that. Every app, every notification, every infinite scroll is a small tax on the one resource that compounds — unbroken attention. The people who learn to protect it have an unfair advantage. The people who don't, don't know what they're missing, which is the point.

The monks knew. The desert fathers called distraction the real enemy — not temptation, not the world, but the scattered mind. The makers know it too: every serious craft, from watchmaking to writing to surgery, demands the same thing the monks demanded. Hours of undivided presence. Different altars, same discipline.

What deep work actually is

It's not just "focusing hard." It's a specific state: cognitively demanding work, performed in long unbroken stretches, with no task-switching, producing something that couldn't be made in fragments. The research is consistent — attention residue (the part of your mind still stuck on the last task) degrades performance for 15-20 minutes after every switch. Four "quick checks" an hour means you're never actually anywhere.

The flip side: a single 90-minute block of true depth outperforms a full day of fragmented effort on anything that matters. Not by a little. The math of compounding applies to cognition too.

The Tuesday-morning version

One defended block daily. Ninety minutes, same time, phone in another room — not silenced, elsewhere. One task. The first week feels like withdrawal; the second feels like remembering something you'd lost.

Ritualize the entry. Same cue, same setup, same start. The monks had bells; you can have a cleared desk and a closed door. Rituals aren't superstition — they're how you tell the nervous system it's time, without spending willpower on the decision.

Measure depth, not hours. At day's end, ask one question: how many unbroken minutes did I give the important thing? Track it. What gets measured gets protected.

Attention is the last scarce resource. Everyone's selling distractions; almost nobody's buying focus. Be the buyer.
