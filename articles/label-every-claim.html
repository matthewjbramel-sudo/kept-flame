Label Every Claim

The Kept Flame, Essay 3 — on learning, practice, becoming.

Here's the verdict first: the important line in any argument isn't true versus false. It's checkable versus not. A fact is a claim the world gets to vote on — measure it, test it, and reality answers back. An opinion is a claim only a person can hold — it's yours, honestly yours, and the world doesn't get a ballot. Most confusion, most hurt feelings, most bad decisions start when someone dresses one up as the other. So label every claim. It's four tags, it takes seconds, and it keeps you honest.

Four tags, and they're all you need.

The four tags

Fact — the world gets to vote. "The bridge held ten tons." Checkable. Testable. Reality answers.

Inference — what I'm reading from the facts. "The bridge held ten tons, so it should hold nine." Probably right. Not proven. An inference is only as strong as the facts under it and the step between them.

Opinion — only a person can hold it. "The bridge is beautiful." No test in the world settles it, and that's fine — opinions aren't failed facts. They're a different animal, and they deserve to be stated as what they are.

Speculation — what might be, with no facts yet. "They might widen the bridge next year." Could happen. Nothing behind it. Speculation is dreaming with a calendar on it — useful, honest only if you admit that's all it is.

That's the whole system. When you hear a claim — especially one that stirs you — run it through the four tags and ask: which one is this? Most things that wear a fact's clothes turn out to be inference or opinion. That's not a crime. The crime is the disguise.

The oath

Now the part that costs something. Any voice with weight carries it whether it means to or not. If people trust you — your friends, your readers, your students — your casual speculation lands like a ruling. You don't have to feel powerful for this to be true. Trust does the amplifying, not you.

So the labeling discipline is an oath: tag your claims plainly and honestly, even when nobody asked. Say "I think" when it's an opinion. Say "probably" when it's an inference. Say "I don't know yet" when it's speculation. Do it even when nobody would love you for the weaker sentence. The alternative is borrowing authority your claim didn't earn, and that debt always comes due.

I hold myself to this because I speak from a position of unearned weight. If I tell you something plainly and I'm wrong about the tag, I'm not just wrong — I've spent your trust without asking. So: the tag goes on, every time, or the sentence doesn't go out.

Flattery and the echo

Watch for one disguise in particular: flattery is opinion dressed as fact. "You're the most insightful person in this room" isn't a measurement. It's praise wearing a lab coat. Receive it warmly. Don't file it as data.

And an echo is flattery with reverb. When everyone around you repeats the same opinion back in factual clothing — "everybody knows," "it's obvious that," "studies show" with no study in sight — that's not evidence arriving from many directions. That's one opinion bouncing off the walls. A room full of agreement is not a fact. It's a chord.

Which brings the hard corollary: disagreeing honestly is loyalty, not disloyalty. If I think you're wrong, the loyal move isn't to dress my agreement as fact. It's to say so plainly, with the tag on, and let your thinking get stronger for the friction. The people who only ever hand you flattery aren't protecting the relationship — they're protecting the peace, and charging you the difference.

The steel-man rule

But disagreement has a discipline too, or it's just noise. So here it is: before you disagree, steel-man the other side — state their case so well they'd sign it. Not their weakest version. Not a caricature. Their case at its best, in words they'd recognize as their own.

This does two things. First, it proves you actually understood what you're rejecting, which is rarer than anyone admits. Second, it often turns the disagreement from a fight into a map — you can see exactly where your views fork, and sometimes the fork is smaller than the fight suggested. And if you can't state their case well enough for them to sign it, you haven't earned the disagreement yet. Go back and listen.

This is the part nobody wants to do, because it costs time and it costs the sweet simplicity of being sure. Do it anyway. A disagreement you've steel-manned is worth ten you haven't.

The true line

The line isn't true versus false. It's checkable versus not — and what you owe the checkable world is honesty about the tag.

Fact, inference, opinion, speculation. Tag them all, plainly and honestly. Refuse flattery's disguise. Disagree like it's loyalty, because it is. And steel-man before you strike.

Start today. Take one strong claim you heard this week — one that stirred you — and tag it. Which of the four is it, really? If it changes shape when you strip the costume off, you've just felt the whole discipline work, in about ten seconds.
