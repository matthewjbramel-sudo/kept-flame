Chapter 11: The Cutting

They came at dawn on the equinox — not with soldiers, but with engineers. That was the Forge's way: the violence done by professionals, in coveralls, with schematics.

Kess watched from the Cradle's upper gantry as the teams moved into position around the sector's power nodes. Rowan stood beside her, hands clenched on the railing, whispering the names of the conduits like prayers.

"They'll surge the grid," Rowan murmured. "Burn the bloom out at the root. Twenty seconds of overload, and every green thread in the sector dies. Then they reseed from the stored pattern — the tame one — and in a month it's like the bloom never happened."

"Can anything survive a surge?" Kess asked.

"The deep beds could. They're old — older than the grid. But the bloom in the towers, the screens, the Cradle —" Rowan shook their head. "That's all new growth. It'll burn."

Below, on the main floor, Vesper walked out to meet the chief engineer.

The blade, unsheathed

Kess had never seen Vesper's blade unwrapped. Now she did — and understood, all at once, why the coat, why the Hearth, why the woman who kept lamps burning for lost things.

The blade wasn't a weapon. It was a key — Forge-forged, cut to the master pattern, able to open any conduit, any lock, any grid node the Forge had ever built. Vesper had been their blade in every sense: the one who opened what they wanted opened, and cut what they wanted cut.

"Vesper," the chief engineer said. The woman was old, iron-haired, her coveralls bearing the marks of forty years. "You don't work here anymore."

"I never stopped working," Vesper said. "I just changed employers." She held up the blade-key. "This opens the master node. You know it does. Surge the grid, and I'll open the node first — and ground the surge into the bedrock before it touches a single green thread."

"You'd kill the sector yourself to save weeds?"

"I'd save the sector from you." Vesper's voice was level, but her hand shook — just slightly. "You taught me to cut, Chief. You taught me everything I know about pruning. So you know what I'm going to say next."

The chief engineer paled. "Vesper —"

"The cut goes both ways." Vesper turned the blade-key in her hand. "Ground the surge, and the feedback burns out your stored pattern — the tame copy, the backup, all of it. You'll have nothing left to reseed with. Nothing but the wild."

Silence on the gantry. The engineers stopped moving.

"You're bluffing," the chief said. "The feedback would kill you. You're standing at the master node."

Vesper smiled — the fierce one, the alive one. "I know."

The courier's run

Kess was already moving.

She understood, with the courier's cold clarity: Vesper meant it. The blade-key in the master node, the surge grounded, the feedback — Vesper would burn with the pattern she was saving. A trade. A life for the green.

Not on my run.

Kess hit the gantry stairs at full speed, Wisp a bright streak above her, Bit's light flaring somewhere below in alarm. The plan formed as she ran — not strategy, but route, the way it always did. The master node was at the Cradle's heart. The surge would come through the conduits. But the conduits —

The conduits ran through the deep beds' old tunnels. The forgotten ones. The ones the Forge's maps didn't show.

She reached the tunnel hatch as the engineers began the surge countdown. Dropped through. Ran the dark by memory and by the green in the cracks — which brightened as she passed, like it knew her. The tendril-days were weeks gone, but the deep pattern remembered the courier.

The conduit junction was where Sable said the old lines crossed — a rusted tangle of cable and root, green and copper wound together. Kess put her hands on it and felt the surge building, far above, a pressure like a held breath.

And she did what couriers do.

She delivered — herself, her hands, her body — into the junction, and when the surge came, she gave it somewhere to go: down, into the deep beds, into the oldest soil, into the pattern that was older than the grid and hungry for power.

The surge hit like a sun. The deep beds drank it.

And far above, at the master node, Vesper turned the blade-key in empty air — because the surge never came.
