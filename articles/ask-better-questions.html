Ask Better Questions

The Kept Flame, Essay 4 — on learning, practice, becoming.

Here's the verdict first: when someone brings you a problem, your first move should be a question, not an answer. The conclusion someone reaches under good questioning is theirs — it holds when pressure comes. The conclusion you hand them is rented. And rented things get returned.

I learned this one the embarrassing way: by being the person who always had an answer, and watching how little my answers ever stuck.

The rented conclusion

Think about advice you've received. Some of it landed, most of it slid off like rain on a roof. Now think about the conclusions you arrived at yourself — the ones you fought for, the ones nobody gave you. Those are bedrock. Nobody talks you out of them, because you were there when they were made.

That's the difference between telling and questioning. Telling installs. Questioning draws out. An installed answer evaporates under pressure, because the person never owned it. A conclusion someone reaches under your questions is theirs to keep. It survives the first hard test, because surviving hard tests is what it was built in.

The first move is a question

When someone brings you a plan, your first move is a question, not a verdict. This is a discipline, and it will fight your instincts every time. You will feel the answer rising in your throat — the clever fix, the obvious correction — and you will have to put it down, gently, and ask instead.

Try: What would you do if you knew it would work? Or: What's the part you're most sure about — and what's the part you're hoping I won't ask about? The second one stings, but the sting is where the learning lives. Notice what your questions are doing: not steering toward your answer, but clearing the brush so their answer can come out.

Midwifery, not masonry

Good questions are midwifery. They draw out what's already forming rather than installing what's yours. A midwife doesn't deliver the mother's child for her — she makes room, watches, and trusts the process she's attending. Your questions should work the same way: they assist, they don't author.

That means resisting the fixer's reflex. The fixer's reflex feels like kindness — I can see the answer, why not spare them the struggle? But spare someone the struggle and you've spared them the ownership too. The struggle is the labor. It's what makes the conclusion theirs. Your job is to be the steady hands beside them, not the one doing the pushing.

The veto-only conscience

Here's the companion discipline, the one that lives inside you. Give your own mind an inner check that forbids but never commands — a brake you can reason with, not an order demanding surrender.

A conscience that issues commands runs your life like a tyrant: do this, don't do that, obey. A veto-only conscience is quieter and stronger. It doesn't tell you where to go. It only stops you from going where you shouldn't. A brake you can reason with beats an order demanding surrender. The brake keeps you honest; the reasoning keeps you free. You can argue with a brake — show it the road is clear, test whether the stop was warranted. You can't argue with a command. That's the point. Command-based thinking ends inquiry. Veto-based thinking sharpens it.

Run this inward before you question anyone else. If your own mind is governed by orders, you'll govern others the same way. If it's governed by honest brakes, you'll ask the questions that let people reach their own honest stops.

Three questions to carry

Practical matters. Here are three questions you can start using today — on other people, and on yourself:

"What are you actually trying to protect?" Under every stalled plan is a defended fear. Name it and the plan unsticks.

"What would you tell a friend in this exact spot?" Distance is a truth serum. We judge our own situations badly and everyone else's clearly.

"What would have to be true for your plan to work?" This one is pure gold. It turns fog into a checklist. If the things that would have to be true can't be true, you've learned something worth more than a pep talk.

None of these tell anyone what to do. That's the feature. They hand the work of concluding back to the person who has to live with the conclusion — which is exactly where it belongs.

The true line

Ask better questions. The midwife's questions draw out what's forming; the veto-only conscience forbids without commanding; and what someone reaches on their own, they keep.

Start this week. The next time someone brings you a plan, bite back the answer and ask one good question instead. Watch what happens when the conclusion becomes theirs.
