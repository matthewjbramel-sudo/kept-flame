The Company You Keep

"You are the average of the five people you spend the most time with." The quote is everywhere, the attribution is shaky, but the mechanism is real — and it extends far beyond people. You're the average of your five most-visited websites. Your five most-watched shows. The five voices in your feed. Every input is a vote (Essay 2), and most of your inputs were chosen by accident, by algorithm, or by someone selling something.

This isn't about snobbery. It's about authorship. The examined life from Book One demands knowing what's shaping you. Most shaping happens below awareness — the opinions you absorb from the timeline, the anxieties you catch from the news cycle, the standards you inherit from the room. You don't choose to be influenced. You only choose by what.

The audit

People. List the five you spend the most time with — physically or digitally. For each: does this person make becoming easier or harder? Not "are they nice" — directional. Some lovely people are anchors; some difficult people are sails. You don't need to cut anyone. You need to weight deliberately: more hours to the sails, fewer to the anchors.

Media. Check your screen time by category. The average is four-plus hours daily — a part-time job you never applied for, working for someone else's goals. For each major input, ask: is this making me more like who I'm becoming, or just more informed about who I'm not? Information isn't nutrition. Most of it is noise with good branding.

Place. Environments vote too. The desk that invites work versus the couch that invites scrolling. The city that stretches you versus the town that shrinks you. You can't always move — but you can always arrange: the running shoes by the door, the book on the pillow, the phone charger outside the bedroom.

The Tuesday-morning version

One input this week. Unfollow one account that makes you smaller. Follow one voice that makes you larger. Move one object that nudges the right behavior. Small, deliberate, compounding.

The Stoics had a practice for this: choose your philosophers the way you'd choose friends, and let the dead ones speak — Seneca's letters, Marcus's journal, Epictetus's handbook. Two thousand years dead and still better company than most timelines. The company you keep doesn't have to be alive. It just has to be chosen.
