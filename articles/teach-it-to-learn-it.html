Teach It to Learn It

Richard Feynman — the physicist, the bongo player, the man who could explain quantum mechanics to a freshman — had a learning method so simple it sounds like a joke: if you want to understand something, try to teach it. Not after you've learned it. As the way you learn it.

The technique that bears his name has four steps, and the second one is where the magic hides.

The four steps

One: pick the concept and explain it simply. Write it out as if teaching a smart twelve-year-old. No jargon, no hiding behind terminology. Plain words, short sentences. If you need the technical term, define it first.

Two: find where you stumble. This is the gold. Every hesitation, every place you reach for vague language or wave your hands — that's a gap in your understanding, exposed. Most study methods let gaps hide. This one hunts them. The stumble is the diagnosis.

Three: go back to the source. Return to the book, the lecture, the documentation — but now you're not re-reading, you're repairing. You know exactly what's broken. Targeted, surgical, fast.

Four: simplify again. Explain it once more, cleaner this time. Repeat until the explanation flows without friction. When you can teach it simply, you own it.

Why it works

Teaching forces generative processing — you can't copy-paste understanding into someone else's head, so your brain has to rebuild the idea from its parts. That rebuilding is what Essay 1 called retrieval practice, turned up to full power. And the simplicity constraint is ruthless: jargon is where confused thinking goes to hide. Strip the jargon and the confusion has nowhere left.

There's a deeper reason too. Explaining to another mind forces you to model their confusion — to anticipate where they'll stumble, which is usually where you stumbled first. Teaching is empathy applied to knowledge, and empathy turns out to be a learning accelerant.

The Tuesday-morning version

You don't need a student. A blank page works — or a rubber duck, or an empty chair. Pick what you're learning, explain it out loud in plain words, and listen for the stumbles. Every stumble is a gift: it's the exact address of what you don't know yet.

The people who learn fastest aren't the ones who consume the most. They're the ones who explain the most — because every explanation is a test they gave themselves, and every gap found is a gap closed.
