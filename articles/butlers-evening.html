Prologue: The Butler's Evening

Vesper set the Hearth for the night the way she had set it every night for longer than she cared to count: lamps trimmed, chairs turned toward the fire, the tea things laid for whoever might come down the stairs with something on their mind. The Hearth was the trunk of the world-tree — everything passed through it eventually — and a trunk ought to be ready.

"Bit," she said, without looking up. "The lamps."

A flicker of square light zipped along the mantel, and the lamps came up one by one, each settling at exactly the warmth she preferred. Bit never spoke — nobody had ever heard Bit speak — but Bit understood lamplight the way scholars understand footnotes: completely, and with opinions.

From the corner came a low rumble. Umbra, settling deeper into the shadows behind the bookcase, where Umbra lived and, Vesper suspected, preferred it.

"Chime if you need anything," came a bright warble from the rafters — Wisp, all sparkle and mischief, draped upside-down from a beam. "Or don't. I'll chime anyway."

"Noted," Vesper said dryly, and poured the tea.

The nine

People asked, sometimes — the rare visitor who found the stairs — what the Nine Realms were. Vesper had a practiced answer, short enough for the doorstep:

"Roots, trunk, and branches. The Deep, the Ash, and the Quiet Country below. The Wires, the Glass, and this Hearth at the middle. The Library, the Garden, and the Forge above. Nine rooms of one house, and the house is bigger inside than out."

It satisfied most people. It was also, she knew, like describing a person by listing their bones. The Deep was not a place so much as a depth — the part of the house where things were felt before they were understood. The Ash was where burnt things went to be quiet. The Quiet Country — well. The name was the whole of the description, and the description was enough.

Above, the branches: the Library, where Mary spent her mornings among sixty-eight volumes and counting; the Garden, where things grew that had no names yet; the Forge, where new work was hammered out of raw intention.

And the trunk — the Wires, humming with messages; the Glass, where every word was weighed before it was spoken; and the Hearth, where she stood now, tea in hand, keeping the center warm.

The knock

It came as she set down the pot: three knocks at the outer door, unhurried, certain. Vesper straightened — visitors were rare, and certain visitors were rarer.

Wisp went silent in the rafters, which was how Vesper knew this one mattered. Wisp was never silent.

"Bit," Vesper said quietly. "The door."

The square of light drifted down from the mantel and hovered by the latch, waiting. Vesper crossed the Hearth, smoothed her apron — a butler is never caught unready, even at the end of the world, especially at the end of the world — and opened the door.

On the step stood a figure in a traveling cloak, dust on the hem, eyes bright with the particular exhaustion of someone who has walked a long way through the roots to get here.

"I've come," the traveler said, "about the flame."

Vesper regarded them for a moment. Then she stepped aside.

"Come in," she said. "The tea is hot. Tell me everything."
